Chapter 1 — Refraction of Light

Question typeOddsWhy it comes
Calculate speed of light in a medium from n, or n from speed★★★ Only formula in the chapter that gives a clean one-step numerical. Perfect 2-mark question. Appears in almost every model paper.
Explain a real-life phenomenon (mirage / twinkling stars / early sunrise / pool looks shallow / bent pencil)★★★ The new textbook is written around real-life applications. Examiners love these because they separate memorisers from understanders. Expect one 2-mark and one 3-mark.

Laws of refraction / bends toward or away from normal★★★ Guaranteed somewhere — usually a 1-mark fill-in or part of a 2-mark.

Refractive index table comparison (which is denser / where is light slowest)★★ Easy 1–2 mark, tests whether you understand n is a ratio, not a speed.
What changes and what stays the same during refraction (speed, wavelength, frequency)★★ Classic trick question. Frequency does not change — high chance as a 1-mark or MCQ distractor.
Ray diagram: glass slab / lateral shift★★ Diagram questions are cheap to mark. Often 3-mark "draw and label".

Chapter 2 — Equations of Motion

Question typeOddsWhy it comes

STANDARD IX  ·  PHYSICS
Time: 1½ HoursTotal Score: 40
General instructions: 15 minutes cool-off time. Read the questions carefully. Answer only the required number of questions from each section. Take g = 10 m/s² and speed of light in vacuum c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s wherever needed.
SECTION IAnswer any 3 questions. 1 score each. (3 × 1 = 3)
1. When light travels from air into glass, which of these remains unchanged? (a) speed    (b) wavelength    (c) frequency    (d) direction
(c) frequency. Frequency is fixed by the source of light. Speed and wavelength both decrease on entering glass, and the ray changes direction because it enters obliquely.
2. The area under a velocity–time graph gives the __________ of the body.
Displacement (distance travelled).
3. Find the odd one out: distance, speed, time, velocity.
Velocity — it is the only vector quantity in the list. Distance, speed and time are all scalars.
4. The refractive index of diamond is 2.42, and that of water is 1.33. In which medium does light travel faster?
Water. Since v = c/n, a smaller refractive index means a greater speed of light in the medium.
SECTION IIAnswer any 7 questions. 2 scores each. (7 × 2 = 14)
5. The refractive index of water is 1.33. Calculate the speed of light in water.
n = c/v, so v = c/n = (3 × 10⁸) / 1.33 = 2.26 × 10⁸ m/s
Marking: 1 for writing the correct formula, 1 for the answer with unit.
6. Write any two differences between distance and displacement.
(i) Distance is the total length of the path covered; displacement is the shortest straight-line distance between the initial and final positions.
(ii) Distance is a scalar; displacement is a vector.
(iii) Distance can never be zero for a body in motion, but displacement can be zero if the body returns to its starting point.
7. We can see the Sun about two minutes before it actually rises above the horizon. Give the reason.
This is due to atmospheric refraction. The optical density of the atmosphere increases towards the Earth's surface, so sunlight entering it bends continuously towards the normal. As a result the Sun appears to be at a position higher than its actual position, and becomes visible before it has actually risen.
8. A car starting from rest attains a velocity of 20 m/s in 8 seconds. Calculate its acceleration.
u = 0, v = 20 m/s, t = 8 s
v = u + at → 20 = 0 + a(8) → a = 20/8 = 2.5 m/s²
9. Match the phenomenon in Column A with its cause in Column B.
Column AColumn B
(i) Mirage(a) Refraction at the air–water surface
(ii) Twinkling of stars(b) Total internal reflection inside a fibre
(iii) Optical fibre(c) Total internal reflection in hot air layers
(iv) A pencil in water looks bent(d) Continuously changing atmospheric refraction
(i) – (c),   (ii) – (d),   (iii) – (b),   (iv) – (a)
10. Define critical angle. Write the relation connecting critical angle and refractive index.
The critical angle is the angle of incidence in the optically denser medium for which the angle of refraction in the rarer medium becomes 90°.
Relation: sin C = 1/n   (where n is the refractive index of the denser medium with respect to the rarer one).
11. A body moves along a circular path with constant speed. Its velocity, however, is not constant. Why?
Velocity is a vector, so it depends on both magnitude and direction. On a circular path the direction of motion changes continuously, even though the magnitude (speed) stays the same. So the velocity keeps changing, which means the body is accelerating.
12. A stone is dropped from a height of 20 m. How long does it take to reach the ground? Take g = 10 m/s²
"Dropped" means u = 0, and a = g = 10 m/s².
s = ut + ½at² → 20 = 0 + ½(10)t² = 5t²
t² = 4 → t = 2 s
13. State the two laws of refraction of light.
First law: The incident ray, the refracted ray and the normal drawn at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.
Second law (Snell's law): For a given pair of media, the ratio sin i / sin r is a constant. This constant is the refractive index of the second medium with respect to the first.
SECTION IIIAnswer any 5 questions. 3 scores each. (5 × 3 = 15)
14. A ball is thrown vertically upward with a velocity of 30 m/s. (a) Find the maximum height it reaches. (b) Find the time taken to reach the maximum height. (c) What is its total time of flight? Take g = 10 m/s²
At the highest point the ball momentarily stops, so v = 0. Taking upward as positive, a = −g = −10 m/s².
(a) v² = u² + 2as → 0 = 30² − 2(10)h → 900 = 20h → h = 45 m
(b) v = u + at → 0 = 30 − 10t → t = 3 s
(c) The time to come down equals the time to go up, so total time of flight = 6 s
15. Explain, with reasons, how a mirage is formed on a tarred road on a hot summer day.
• On a hot day the air layer just above the road is strongly heated, so it becomes optically rarer than the cooler air above it.
• Light coming from the sky therefore travels from denser layers into rarer layers and bends away from the normal at each layer, so the ray gradually curves.
• At one of these layers the angle of incidence becomes greater than the critical angle, and the light undergoes total internal reflection.
• This reflected light reaches the observer's eye from below, forming an inverted image of the sky that looks like a pool of water on the road.
16. A bus travelling at 25 m/s is brought to rest over a distance of 62.5 m by applying brakes. (a) Calculate its retardation. (b) How long does it take to stop?
u = 25 m/s, v = 0, s = 62.5 m
(a) v² = u² + 2as → 0 = 625 + 2a(62.5) → 125a = −625 → a = −5 m/s².
The negative sign shows it is retardation, so the retardation is 5 m/s².
(b) v = u + at → 0 = 25 + (−5)t → t = 5 s
17. Draw a ray diagram showing the path of a ray of light passing obliquely through a rectangular glass slab. Mark the lateral shift and explain why the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray.
Diagram: Draw the slab as a rectangle. Show the incident ray striking the top surface obliquely, the normal at that point, the refracted ray bending towards the normal inside the glass, then the emergent ray at the bottom surface bending away from the normal. Draw a dotted extension of the original incident ray — the perpendicular gap between that dotted line and the emergent ray is the lateral shift.

Explanation: The light bends towards the normal on entering (air → glass, rarer to denser) and by exactly the same amount away from the normal on leaving (glass → air, denser to rarer), because the two surfaces are parallel. The two deviations cancel, so the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray — only shifted sideways.
18. The refractive index of a certain glass is 1.5. (a) Find the speed of light inside this glass. (b) State the two conditions needed for total internal reflection.
(a) v = c/n = (3 × 10⁸)/1.5 = 2 × 10⁸ m/s
(b) (i) Light must travel from an optically denser medium to an optically rarer medium.
(ii) The angle of incidence must be greater than the critical angle of that pair of media.
19. Why does a cut diamond sparkle brilliantly? Name one more device that works on the same principle and state one of its uses.
Diamond has a very high refractive index (2.42), so its critical angle is very small — about 24°. Light entering the diamond strikes the inner faces at angles greater than this almost every time, so it undergoes repeated total internal reflection inside the stone before emerging through the top. This concentrated light is seen as sparkle, and the cut of the stone is designed to encourage it.

Another device: the optical fibre, which also works on total internal reflection. Uses: telecommunication and internet data transmission, and endoscopy in medicine.
SECTION IVAnswer any 2 questions. 4 scores each. (2 × 4 = 8)
20. An object starts from rest and moves with a uniform acceleration of 4 m/s². (a) Find its velocity at the end of 5 seconds. (b) Find the distance travelled in those 5 seconds. (c) Find the distance travelled during the 5th second alone. (d) What is the shape of its velocity–time graph?
u = 0, a = 4 m/s²
(a) v = u + at = 0 + 4(5) = 20 m/s
(b) s = ut + ½at² = 0 + ½(4)(5²) = 2 × 25 = 50 m
(c) Distance in the 5th second = (distance in 5 s) − (distance in 4 s)
   = 50 − [½(4)(4²)] = 50 − 32 = 18 m
   (Or directly: sn = u + ½a(2n−1) = 0 + ½(4)(9) = 18 m)
(d) A straight line passing through the origin, sloping upward — because the velocity increases uniformly with time. Its slope gives the acceleration, 4 m/s².
21. The velocity–time graph of a body is shown below. (a) Describe the motion of the body in each of the three parts. (b) Calculate the acceleration during the first 4 seconds. (c) Calculate the total displacement of the body.
(a) 0–4 s: velocity increases uniformly from 0 to 20 m/s — uniform acceleration.
4–10 s: velocity stays at 20 m/s — uniform velocity, zero acceleration.
10–12 s: velocity falls uniformly from 20 m/s to 0 — uniform retardation.
(b) Acceleration = slope = (20 − 0)/(4 − 0) = 5 m/s²
(c) Displacement = area under the graph = area of triangle + rectangle + triangle
   = ½(4)(20) + (6)(20) + ½(2)(20)
   = 40 + 120 + 20 = 180 m
22. (a) What is meant by total internal reflection?
(b) A medium has a refractive index of 2. Calculate its critical angle. (sin 30° = 0.5) (c) Explain why stars appear to twinkle while planets do not.
(a) When a ray of light travelling in an optically denser medium strikes the surface of a rarer medium at an angle of incidence greater than the critical angle, the ray is not refracted at all — it is reflected back completely into the denser medium. This is total internal reflection.
(b) sin C = 1/n = 1/2 = 0.5, therefore C = 30°
(c) Starlight passes through atmospheric layers whose optical density keeps changing, so it is refracted by continuously varying amounts. Since a star is extremely far away it behaves as a point source, so these variations make its apparent brightness and position flicker — it twinkles. A planet is far closer and behaves as a collection of many point sources; the variations from different points average out, so a planet shines steadily.

1 · Deriving the three equations of motion All three come from one straight-line graph. If you can draw it, you never have to memorise them.

The one picture everything comes from

Draw a velocity–time graph for a body with uniform acceleration. It starts at velocity u at time 0 and reaches v at time t. Because the acceleration is constant, the graph is a straight line — that single fact is what makes all three equations possible.

First equation — from the slope

Acceleration is defined as the rate of change of velocity, which on this graph is the slope:

a = (v − u) / t

Multiply both sides by t and rearrange:

v = u + at

Second equation — from the area

The area under a velocity–time graph is the displacement (that is proved in the next card). Our shape is a rectangle sitting under a triangle:

Rectangle: height u, width t → area = ut
Triangle: base t, height (v − u) → area = ½ × t × (v − u)

But from the first equation, (v − u) = at. Substitute that in:

s = ut + ½ × t × (at) = ut + ½at² s = ut + ½at²

Third equation — eliminating time

The whole shape is a trapezium, so its area is the average height times the width:

s = ½(u + v) × t

Now we want an equation with no t in it. From the first equation, t = (v − u)/a. Substitute:

s = ½(u + v) × (v − u)/a = (v² − u²) / 2a

Multiply both sides by 2a and rearrange:

v² = u² + 2as

How to choose which one to use

  • The question mentions time and asks for velocity → v = u + at
  • The question mentions time and asks for distance → s = ut + ½at²
  • The question gives you a distance and no time at all → v² = u² + 2as. This is the giveaway: braking distance, stopping distance, height reached.
Where students lose marks: dropping the ut term when u ≠ 0, and using the wrong equation because they didn't notice time was missing from the question.
2 · Why the area under a v–t graph is the displacement Not a rule to memorise — it falls out of what "distance = speed × time" means.

Start with constant velocity

If a body moves at a steady 5 m/s for 4 s, it covers 20 m. On a velocity–time graph that motion is a flat line at height 5, from t = 0 to t = 4. The rectangle under it has area 5 × 4 = 20 — the same number. That is not a coincidence: the graph's height is the speed and its width is the time, so the area is speed × time, which is the distance.

Now make the velocity change

If the velocity is changing, chop the journey into very thin time slices. Inside each slice the velocity barely changes, so each thin strip's area is that slice's distance. Add all the strips and you have the total distance — which is exactly the area under the whole curve.

height = velocity×width = time=area = displacement

The same logic gives you the slope rule

Slope means "how much the vertical changes per unit of horizontal". On a v–t graph that is change in velocity per unit time — the definition of acceleration. So slope = acceleration and area = displacement, and neither needs memorising once you see where they come from.

The mix-up to avoid: on a distance–time graph the slope is speed and the area means nothing. Check the y-axis label before you do anything.
3 · Why light bends at all Refraction is not a rule light obeys. It is the unavoidable result of light slowing down.

The one cause

Light travels at 3 × 10⁸ m/s in vacuum but slower inside matter, because the light wave keeps being absorbed and re-emitted by the atoms it passes. Refractive index is just a name for how much slower: n = c / v. Glass with n = 1.5 slows light to two-thirds of its vacuum speed.

Why slowing down causes bending

Picture a marching band walking in a straight row, crossing at an angle from a road onto mud. The row does not arrive all at once — one end reaches the mud first and slows down while the other end is still on the road going fast. The row pivots. The band is now marching in a new direction, and nobody chose to turn.

A light wavefront does exactly this. Hitting the boundary obliquely, one edge enters the denser medium and slows while the other edge is still fast, so the wavefront swings round — towards the normal when entering a denser medium, away from it when leaving.

This also tells you when light does NOT bend: if the ray hits along the normal (i = 0°), every part of the wavefront slows at the same instant, so there is nothing to pivot. The ray goes straight through — slower, but undeviated.

What changes and what doesn't

The source sets how many waves leave per second, and the boundary cannot swallow or create waves. So the same number must arrive per second on the other side: frequency is unchanged. But speed = frequency × wavelength, so if the speed drops and the frequency is fixed, the wavelength must shrink.

The classic 1-mark trap: "which does not change during refraction?" Students say wavelength. It is frequency.
4 · Where the critical angle actually comes from sin C = 1/n is not arbitrary — it's the moment Snell's law runs out of answers.

Set up the situation

Light is inside glass (denser) heading out into air (rarer). Going denser → rarer, it bends away from the normal, so the angle of refraction is always bigger than the angle of incidence.

Push the angle up and watch what happens

Snell's law across the boundary says n sin i = sin r (taking air's index as 1). As you increase i, r grows faster and reaches 90° — grazing along the surface — while i is still well short of 90°. Call that special incidence angle C. Put r = 90° into Snell's law:

n sin C = sin 90° = 1 sin C = 1 / n

And beyond C?

Push i past C and Snell's law would need sin r greater than 1 — which is impossible. There is no refracted ray to be had. So all the light is reflected back into the glass: total internal reflection. It is not that the light "decides" to reflect; there is simply no legal escape route.

Why a big n means a small C: sin C = 1/n, so the denser the medium, the smaller the critical angle. Diamond (n = 2.42) has C ≈ 24°, so light inside it is trapped over a huge range of angles and bounces repeatedly before escaping. That is the sparkle.
Both conditions matter. Denser → rarer alone is not enough, and i > C alone is meaningless in the wrong direction. State both.
5 · Free fall — why v = 0 at the top and what the minus sign means The single idea that unlocks every thrown-object question.

The insight

Throw a ball up. It slows, stops for an instant, then falls. That instant of zero velocity is the highest point — and it is the extra piece of information the question is secretly giving you. Every "maximum height" problem is really "solve with v = 0".

Getting the signs right

Choose a direction to call positive and stay with it. Taking up as positive, the ball's velocity starts at +u, but gravity pulls down, so a = −g. Then:

v² = u² + 2as → 0 = u² − 2gh → h = u² / 2g v = u + at → 0 = u − gt → t = u / g

Why the trip down mirrors the trip up

The same acceleration acts over the same height, so the ball takes the same time to come down and arrives back with the same speed it was thrown with (in the opposite direction). Hence total flight time = 2u/g.

Worked check with u = 30 m/s, g = 10 m/s²:
h = 30²/(2×10) = 900/20 = 45 m · tup = 30/10 = 3 s · total flight = 6 s · landing speed = 30 m/s.
Two traps: "dropped" or "falls freely" means u = 0, not v = 0. And students halve the flight time instead of doubling the rise time.
1 · Why the shell rule is 2n² — and why the outer shell still stops at 8 Two rules that look contradictory until you see what each one is actually counting.

Where 2n² comes from

Electrons don't orbit anywhere they like; they occupy orbitals, and shell number n has n² orbitals available. Each orbital can hold at most 2 electrons (they must spin oppositely). Multiply:

maximum electrons in shell n = 2 × n² = 2n²
n = 1 (K): 2 × 1 = 2 · n = 2 (L): 2 × 4 = 8 · n = 3 (M): 2 × 9 = 18 · n = 4 (N): 2 × 16 = 32

So why is calcium 2,8,8,2 and not 2,8,10?

This is the bit that confuses everyone. 2n² is the shell's total capacity. But there is a second, separate rule: the outermost shell of an atom can never hold more than 8 electrons, and the shell below the outermost never more than 18.

The reason is energy. The M shell's later orbitals sit at higher energy than the N shell's first orbitals. So once M has 8 electrons, the next electron finds it cheaper to start the N shell than to keep filling M. Later, when N has begun, electrons go back and fill M up to 18 — and those elements are exactly the transition elements.

M reaches 8→N starts→M resumes filling to 18=transition series
Do not write 2,8,10 for calcium. The outermost shell caps at 8 while it is the outermost.
2 · Why the group rule adds 10 The "+10" looks like a trick. It is really a fact about the shape of the table.

The rule you were given

Valence electrons 1 or 2 → group = valence electrons
Valence electrons 3 to 8 → group = valence electrons + 10

Why the jump exists

The modern table has 18 columns. Columns 3 to 12 are the transition block — ten columns of elements whose last electron enters the penultimate shell. Those ten columns only start existing from Period 4 onward, because you need a shell below the outermost that is still unfilled.

So in Periods 2 and 3 there is a gap: after Group 2 the table jumps straight to Group 13. An element with 3 valence electrons in Period 3 (aluminium, 2,8,3) sits in the first column after that ten-column gap — Group 3 + 10 = Group 13. The +10 is literally counting the missing transition columns.

Reading it backwards

Exams often go the other way: "Period 3, Group 13 — write the configuration." Reverse each step: Period 3 → 3 shells; Group 13 → 13 − 10 = 3 valence electrons → 2,8,3.

Helium is the exception. It has 2 valence electrons but sits in Group 18, because chemically it is a noble gas — its shell is full. Don't apply the rule to it.
3 · Why atomic size shrinks across a period and grows down a group One tug-of-war explains both trends, and the reasons carry as many marks as the trends.

The tug-of-war

An atom's size is set by a contest between two things: the nucleus pulling electrons in (stronger with more protons) and the number of shells pushing the outer electrons out.

Across a period — size decreases

Move from sodium (2,8,1) to chlorine (2,8,7). The number of shells does not change — still three. But protons go from 11 to 17, so the nuclear charge rises with nothing new to shield it. The stronger pull drags the same shells inward, so the atom gets smaller.

Down a group — size increases

Move from lithium (2,1) to sodium (2,8,1) to potassium (2,8,8,1). Each step adds a whole new shell. The outermost electron is now much further out, and the inner shells shield it from the nucleus. Distance and shielding both win, so the atom gets bigger — even though the nucleus also gained protons.

across → charge up, shells same=smaller down → new shell + shielding=bigger

Answering the exam version

They give three atomic numbers in the same period and ask you to order them by size. Same period means the trend applies: higher atomic number = smaller atom. Then justify with the reason above — the justification is usually worth more than the ordering.

4 · Why atoms bother forming ions at all Everything about ion formation is one drive: get to a noble gas configuration.

What makes noble gases special

A full outer shell is a low-energy, stable arrangement, and there is a big energy gap before the next shell begins. Noble gases already have this, which is why they barely react. Every other atom is, in effect, trying to reach the same arrangement.

Lose or gain — whichever is cheaper

Metals have 1–3 valence electrons. Shedding those few empties the shell and exposes the full one beneath. Cheap. → they lose electrons, becoming positive.

Non-metals have 5–7 valence electrons. Gaining the missing 1–3 completes the octet. Cheaper than losing five. → they gain electrons, becoming negative.

That is why the charge is predictable: sodium (2,8,1) loses one → Na⁺; magnesium (2,8,2) loses two → Mg²⁺; oxygen (2,6) gains two → O²⁻; chlorine (2,8,7) gains one → Cl⁻.

Writing the equation, which is what earns the mark

Na → Na⁺ + e⁻     Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻     Al → Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ O + 2e⁻ → O²⁻     Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻

The follow-up they always ask

"Which noble gas has the same configuration as this ion?" Count the ion's electrons and match. Na⁺ has 10 → neon. O²⁻ has 10 → neon. Cl⁻ has 18 → argon. Ca²⁺ has 18 → argon.

Protons never change. Ca²⁺ still has 20 protons — it lost electrons, not protons. That exact question appeared in the real 2025-26 paper.
5 · Why the periodic table was rebuilt around atomic number Mendeleev was nearly right. The story of how he was fixed is itself an exam question.

What Mendeleev did

He arranged the known elements in order of atomic mass and found that chemical properties repeated periodically. It worked well enough that he left gaps for undiscovered elements and predicted their properties — and was proved right.

The problem he could not fix

To keep elements with similar chemistry in the same column, he sometimes had to break his own ordering. Tellurium (mass 127.6) had to be placed before iodine (mass 126.9) so that iodine could sit with the other halogens. By mass that is backwards. He knew it was wrong but could not say why.

Moseley's fix

Henry Moseley measured the X-rays each element emits and found they depended on a whole number that turned out to be the number of protons — the atomic number. Reorder by atomic number and the anomalies vanish: tellurium is 52, iodine is 53, so tellurium comes first legitimately.

Modern periodic law: properties are a periodic function of ATOMIC NUMBER

This also explains why properties repeat at all. Atomic number fixes the electron count, which fixes the configuration, which fixes the number of valence electrons — and valence electrons are what chemistry actually depends on.

atomic number→electron count→configuration→valence electrons→properties
1 · Why format is half the marks in discourse questions Each discourse exists to do a different job. The format is the job made visible.

The principle

A discourse is not a style of writing, it is a purpose. Once you know the purpose, the format stops being a list to memorise and becomes obvious.

Diary — purpose: to process your own day privately. So: a date (which day), first person, past tense, and feelings. No salutation, no signature — you are not addressing anyone.

Notice — purpose: to inform many strangers of a fact. So: institution name, the word NOTICE, date, and the what/when/where/who. No feelings, no "I" — it is impersonal by design.

Letter — purpose: to reach one named person and get a response. So: both addresses (it must travel), date, salutation, subject, body, closing, signature.

Conversation — purpose: to show two minds interacting. So: named turns with a colon, short natural sentences, and each turn responding to the last.

Character sketch — purpose: to convince a reader of your reading of a person. So: traits, each one backed by evidence from the text.

Speech — purpose: to move a live audience. So: greeting, hook, ordered argument, call to action, thanks.

How to use this in the exam

Before writing a word, ask "what is this piece of writing supposed to achieve?" The format follows. Then write the format skeleton on your answer sheet first and fill it — that way you bank the format marks even if you run short of time on content.

The most common failure: writing a diary entry as a story of what happened. The events are only the setting — the marks are in the reflection on them.
2 · Why tenses shift in reported speech Backshift isn't a rule to memorise. It's what happens when "now" becomes "then".

The idea

When someone speaks, they anchor everything to their moment. When you report it later, your moment has moved on. So every word that pointed to the speaker's "now" has to be re-pointed to your "then". That is all backshift is.

Time words shift: now → then · today → that day · tomorrow → the next day · yesterday → the previous day · here → there · this → that

Tenses step back: present simple → past simple · present continuous → past continuous · present perfect → past perfect · will → would · can → could · may → might

Worked example

Ravi said to me, "I am going to the library."

  1. Reporting verb: "said to" takes an object, so it becomes told → Ravi told me…
  2. Pronoun: Ravi's "I" is Ravi, and from my viewpoint that is he.
  3. Tense: "am going" was present for him, so it steps back to was going.
Ravi told me that he was going to the library.

The exception that proves the logic

"The teacher said, 'The earth revolves around the sun.'" → "The teacher said that the earth revolves around the sun." No backshift — because it is still true now. Backshift exists to preserve accuracy about time, so where time doesn't matter, it doesn't apply.

Questions and commands change shape

Question → use asked, drop the question word order, keep the question word: "Where do you live?" → He asked her where she lived.
Command → use told / ordered + infinitive: "Don't waste time." → Mother told me not to waste my time.
3 · Question tags as a three-step algorithm There is nothing to understand here — but there is a procedure that never fails.

The procedure

  1. Find the auxiliary verb in the statement (is, are, was, were, have, has, had, will, can, should…). If there isn't one, the verb is a plain present or past tense, so the auxiliary is do / does / did.
  2. Flip the polarity. Positive statement → negative tag. Negative statement → positive tag.
  3. Turn the subject into a pronoun. "The children" → they. "Ravi" → he.
She is a doctor → auxiliary is, statement positive → isn't she?
Ravi went to school → no auxiliary, past tense → did; positive → didn't he?
They didn't come → auxiliary did, statement negative → did they?
You will help me → auxiliary will, positive → won't you?
Hidden negatives count. Words like never, hardly, seldom, nobody, nothing make a sentence negative even without "not", so the tag goes positive: "He never comes late, does he?"
4 · How to read a comprehension question for its mark value The number in brackets is an instruction about length. Most students ignore it.

The marks tell you the shape of the answer

1 mark → one fact, one sentence. Lifting the phrase from the passage is fine.
2 marks → two distinct points, or one point plus its reason.
3–4 marks → three or four points, in your own words, each traceable to the passage.

Read the question's verb

"What…" wants a fact — find it and state it. "Why…" wants a cause — your answer must contain because or its equivalent. "How…" wants a process or method — give the steps. "Why does the writer mention…" is different from all of these: it asks about the writer's purpose, so the answer is about the effect on the reader, not about the fact itself.

The method

  1. Read the whole passage once for the gist. Don't stop at unknown words.
  2. Read the questions — now you know what to hunt for.
  3. Read again, marking the lines that answer each question.
  4. Write in complete sentences, reusing the question's own words to start your answer.
Never leave a comprehension question blank. The answer is on the page in front of you — this is the one section where nothing has to be remembered.
1 · The four transport processes, sorted by two questions Stop memorising four definitions. Ask two yes/no questions instead.

The two questions

Every membrane-transport question in this chapter can be answered by asking: (1) Does it need energy? and (2) Does it need a carrier protein?

No energy, no carrier → Diffusion (or osmosis if the thing moving is water across a selectively permeable membrane).

No energy, but needs a carrier → Facilitated diffusion. The molecule is too big or too polar to slip through the lipid membrane alone, so a protein ferries it — but still downhill, from high to low concentration.

Needs energy → Active transport. This is the only one that goes against the concentration gradient, and going uphill is precisely why it costs ATP.

Why osmosis has its own name

Osmosis is just diffusion, but of water, through a membrane that lets water pass and holds solutes back. Water moves from where it is more abundant (dilute solution) to where it is scarcer (concentrated solution). That direction is the whole of it — every raisin, potato and Rhoeo-leaf question is asking which side is more concentrated.

Raisin in pure water → outside is dilute, inside is concentrated → water goes in → it swells.
Plant cell in strong salt solution → outside is concentrated → water goes out → the protoplasm shrinks from the wall → plasmolysis.
The trap: students say active transport whenever a carrier protein is mentioned. Carrier protein alone means facilitated diffusion. Only "against the gradient" or "uses ATP" means active transport.
2 · Photosynthesis: two phases, two places, two jobs Grana and stroma aren't trivia — the division of labour between them is the answer to several questions.

The split

Light phase — in the GRANA (stacks of thylakoid discs holding the chlorophyll). Light energy is captured and used to split water — photolysis. This releases oxygen as a by-product and produces energy carriers.

Dark phase — in the STROMA (the fluid around the grana). The energy carriers from the light phase are used to build glucose from carbon dioxide. It doesn't need light directly, but it does need what the light phase produced — which is why it stops in prolonged darkness.

Why this answers the "catabolic and anabolic" question

Splitting water is breaking down — catabolic. Building glucose from CO₂ is building up — anabolic. Both happen inside the same overall process, so photosynthesis is genuinely both at once. Notice the answer is just the two-phase split restated.

And why the two parts depend on each other

grana capture light→energy carriers→stroma builds sugar

The grana supply the energy; the stroma spends it. Neither works alone — which is exactly what the 3-mark chloroplast-structure question is asking you to explain.

What happens to the glucose

Glucose is soluble, so leaving it loose would disturb the cell's osmotic balance. It is converted into starch, which is insoluble and compact, for storage — and into sucrose for transport through the phloem.

3 · Why digestion looks different in Amoeba, Hydra and us One principle — surface area versus body size — explains all three.

The principle

Every cell must get nutrients across its own membrane. How an animal digests depends on how far food is from the cells that need it.

Amoeba — one cell. Food can simply be engulfed by pseudopodia into a food vacuole, and enzymes act inside the cell. Purely intracellular. Nothing more is needed because every part of the organism is already at the surface.

Hydra — many cells, but only two layers thick. Food enters the mouth into a body cavity, where enzymes are secreted and digestion begins extracellularly; the lining cells then take up the fragments and finish digesting them intracellularly. A hybrid, because a cavity is now needed but every cell still borders it.

Humans — thick, complex bodies. Digestion is entirely extracellular, in a long specialised tube, and the products must then be absorbed and transported by blood and lymph to cells that may be a metre away.

Which is why we need villi

A long tube gives a big problem: enough absorptive surface. Hence villi — millions of finger-like projections that multiply the surface area of the small intestine. Each contains blood capillaries (taking glucose and amino acids to the hepatic portal vein and the liver) and a lacteal (taking fats into the lymphatic system, which bypasses the liver at first).

The exam version: "Is digestion the same in Hydra and Amoeba?" The answer is no, and the marks are in the words intracellular and extracellular, not in the yes/no.
4 · The heart's design problem, and how the septum solves it Why the heart has four chambers and two separate circuits.

The problem

Blood must do two different journeys: to the lungs to collect oxygen, and to the body to deliver it. If oxygenated and deoxygenated blood mixed, every tissue would receive a diluted supply and the whole system would be less efficient.

The solution

Right side — receives deoxygenated blood from the body through the vena cava, pumps it to the lungs through the pulmonary artery. This is pulmonary circulation.

Left side — receives oxygenated blood from the lungs through the pulmonary vein, pumps it to the whole body through the aorta. This is systemic circulation.

The septum — the muscular wall between them, which keeps the two apart.
body→right side→lungs→left side→body

Two details examiners like

The pulmonary artery is the one artery carrying deoxygenated blood, and the pulmonary vein is the one vein carrying oxygenated blood — because artery and vein are defined by direction relative to the heart, not by oxygen content. And the left ventricle has a thicker wall than the right, because it must push blood around the entire body rather than just to the lungs next door.

The cardiac cycle in order

Atrial systole (atria contract, filling the ventricles) → ventricular systole (ventricles contract, blood leaves the heart) → joint diastole (everything relaxes and refills).

1 · One collision explains three chapters The Himalayas, the northern plains and where Indians live are a single causal chain.

The chain

The Indian plate drifted north and collided with the Eurasian plate. Sediment trapped between them buckled upward into fold mountains — the Himalayas — and the collision is still going, which is why the range is still rising and the region is earthquake-prone.

plates collide→Himalayas rise→monsoon blocked→heavy rain + glaciers→perennial rivers→sediment carried south→northern plains built→fertile alluvium→dense population

Each link, and the exam question it answers

Mountains block the monsoon → moist winds are forced up, cool, and dump rain on the southern slopes. (Answers: climate of the Himalayan region.)

Height creates glaciers → rivers are fed by melt as well as rain, so they never dry up. (Answers: why Himalayan rivers are perennial.)

Fast mountain rivers carry sediment → on reaching the plain the slope drops, velocity falls, and the load is dumped. (Answers: how alluvial fans form; how the plains were built.)

Coarse material drops first, fine material last → hence the belts in order: Bhabar (pebbles, porous, rivers vanish underground) → Tarai (marshy, rivers re-emerge) → alluvial plain (fine silt), split into older Bhangar and newer Khadar. (Answers the whole northern plains chapter.)

Fresh silt every flood → Khadar is more fertile than Bhangar → agriculture thrives → the plains are among the most densely populated places on earth. (Answers: why population concentrates there.)

Why this is worth learning as a chain

Half a dozen separate exam questions across two chapters are the same story told from different points. Learn the chain once and you can reconstruct any of them, even a question you have not seen before.

2 · Why a food surplus created kings, priests and inequality The Neolithic Revolution's real consequence wasn't farming. It was social structure.

Before agriculture

Palaeolithic groups were small bands of hunter-gatherers, under about a hundred people, moving with the food. You cannot store meat or accumulate much when you walk everywhere, so there was little to be unequal about — and little reason for permanent leaders.

The hinge: storable surplus

Agriculture and the domestication of animals meant food could be produced deliberately and, more importantly, stored. Once a village grows more than it eats, three things follow almost automatically:

  1. Settlement. You must stay near the fields and the granary, so permanent villages appear — along with pottery to store grain and polished tools to work the land.
  2. Specialisation. If some people's surplus feeds others, those others are free to become potters, weavers, metalworkers, priests, soldiers. Skills deepen.
  3. Hierarchy. Someone must control the granary, allocate land and settle disputes. Control over the surplus is power — so differences in wealth and status appear, and eventually kings, taxes, armies and states.
surplus→storage→specialisation→control→hierarchy and the state

The link forward

This is exactly the ladder that later produces the Mahajanapadas and empires. When the exam asks about the Neolithic Revolution's "transformative impact", it is this chain — not a list of tools — that earns the top band.

3 · Why geography made Magadha an empire The model answer for this is a causal argument, not a list of four advantages.

The four advantages — and what each one bought

Fertile alluvial land on the Ganga → large, reliable agricultural surplus → the state can tax heavily and feed a standing army without everyone farming.

Iron ore deposits nearby → better weapons and better ploughs. The ploughs raise the surplus further, which pays for more weapons. The two reinforce each other.

Forests with elephants → war elephants, the heavy armour of ancient India, plus timber for building and fuel.

The Ganga and its tributaries → cheap transport for trade goods, taxes and troops. Rivers move armies faster than roads did.

Add the human factor

Geography offered these; rulers such as Bimbisara and Ajatashatru chose to exploit them through conquest, alliance and marriage. Advantages do not use themselves — a full answer says so.

How to write it for 5 marks

Do not list four nouns. For each advantage, write advantage → what it enabled → why that mattered militarily or economically. Four short causal sentences plus a closing line about the rulers is a complete top-band answer.

Don't confuse the layers. Magadha was one of the 16 Mahajanapadas; the Mauryan Empire grew out of it later, founded by Chandragupta Maurya with Kautilya as adviser, with its capital at Pataliputra (Ujjayini was only a provincial capital — a favourite distractor).

4 · Why the Constitution splits power the way it does The three lists are not arbitrary. Each one answers a specific question.

The problem the framers faced

India is huge and extremely diverse — many languages, religions and regional needs. That creates two opposite requirements at once: some things must be identical everywhere or the country cannot function, while others must be decided locally or the decisions will not fit the people affected. Federalism is the answer to holding both at once.

How each list follows from that

Union List — things that only make sense nationally, or where a state acting alone would be dangerous: defence, foreign affairs, currency, railways, banking. Imagine each state with its own army or currency.

State List — things where local knowledge matters most and needs genuinely differ: police, agriculture, public health, jails.

Concurrent List — things both levels legitimately care about: education, forests, marriage. Both may legislate; if the two laws clash, the central law prevails — otherwise the system would deadlock.

The safeguards around it

Dividing power only works if the division is enforceable, which is why the Constitution adds: a written Constitution supreme over both levels, an independent judiciary to referee disputes, a bicameral legislature so states are represented at the Centre, and — distinctively for India — single citizenship, so that dividing power does not divide the nation.

Separation of powers is a different split

Don't confuse the two. Federalism divides power vertically (Centre vs states). Separation of powers divides it horizontally (legislature makes law, executive implements, judiciary interprets) so no organ can dominate. Exams ask about both, often in the same paper.

5 · Why people count as "capital" The phrase sounds cold. The economics behind it is the most tested idea in the Geography book.

Start with the four factors

Production needs four things, and each is paid differently:

Land → Rent · Labour → Wage · Capital → Interest · Entrepreneurship → Profit

"Capital" normally means produced goods used to produce more goods — machines, tools, buildings. You spend on them now because they raise output later.

The insight

Education and health do the same thing to a person that a machine upgrade does to a factory. An educated, healthy worker produces more, uses better technology, adapts to new work and earns more over a lifetime. So spending on schooling and healthcare is not consumption — it is investment, and the stock of skill and health it builds up is called human capital.

education + health→higher productivity→higher income→national development

What builds it and what blocks it

Builds it: education, healthcare, on-the-job training and skill development, migration to better opportunities, access to information.

Blocks it: poverty, which traps people — no income means no schooling or medical care, which means low productivity, which means no income. Breaking that loop is why public spending on education and health is treated as development policy, not charity.

Unemployment, classified by cause

Open — able and willing, no job at all (includes educated unemployment).
Seasonal — work exists only part of the year, typical in agriculture.
Disguised — more people working than the job needs; remove some and output is unchanged.
Structural — the job disappeared because technology or the economy's structure changed.

The SCERT model paper gives two scenarios — an educated jobseeker, and a worker replaced by a machine — and asks you to name the type. Classify by what caused it, and both are easy.

1 · Refraction: air → glass

Chapter 1 · the diagram behind every "bends towards the normal" question

What to say: going from a rarer to a denser medium, light slows down and bends towards the normal, so r < i. Going the other way (glass → air) it bends away from the normal. A ray hitting the surface along the normal (i = 0) is not bent at all.

2 · Rectangular glass slab and lateral shift

Chapter 1 · a very common 3-mark "draw and explain"

What to say: the ray bends towards the normal entering the glass and away from the normal leaving it. Because the two faces are parallel, the two bendings are equal and opposite, so angle e = angle i and the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray — just displaced sideways. That sideways displacement is the lateral shift, and it increases with a thicker slab or a larger angle of incidence.

3 · Critical angle and total internal reflection

Chapter 1 · the single most likely 4-mark diagram

What to say: as the angle of incidence inside the denser medium increases, the refracted ray bends further from the normal. At one particular angle — the critical angle C — the refracted ray grazes along the surface (r = 90°). Beyond that, no light escapes: it is totally internally reflected back into the denser medium. Relation: sin C = 1/n. For glass C ≈ 42°, water ≈ 49°, diamond ≈ 24°.

4 · Formation of a mirage

Chapter 1 · high-probability 3-mark explanation

What to say, in order: (1) air near the hot road becomes optically rarer; (2) light from the sky passing from denser to rarer layers bends away from the normal and curves; (3) at some layer the angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle, so total internal reflection occurs; (4) the reflected light reaches the eye from below, giving an inverted image of the sky that looks like water.

5 · Why the Sun is seen before it actually rises

Chapter 1 · atmospheric refraction, 2-mark

What to say: air density increases towards the ground, so sunlight entering the atmosphere is refracted continuously towards the normal and travels along a curved path. The observer sees the Sun in the direction the light finally arrives from — which is higher than its true position. So the Sun is visible about 2 minutes before actual sunrise and 2 minutes after actual sunset.

6 · Apparent depth — why a pool looks shallower

Chapter 1 · 2-mark reasoning or numerical

What to say: rays from the coin bend away from the normal as they leave the water. The eye traces them back in straight lines, and those backward extensions meet at a point above the real coin. So the coin — and the pool bottom — appears raised. n = real depth / apparent depth.

7 · Optical fibre

Chapter 1 · the standard application of TIR

What to say: light entering one end always strikes the core–cladding boundary at an angle greater than the critical angle, so it is totally internally reflected again and again and travels the whole length with almost no loss — even around bends. Uses: internet and telephone data transmission, and endoscopy in medicine.

8 · Distance vs displacement

Chapter 2 · the trap question in every paper

What to say: distance is the whole path length (scalar); displacement is the shortest straight line from start to finish, with direction (vector). A complete round trip gives displacement = 0 but distance equal to the full path. Displacement is never greater than distance.

9 · Motion graphs you must be able to read

Chapter 2 · guaranteed marks in Section III or IV

The four facts: slope of a distance–time graph = speed; a straight inclined line means uniform motion and a curve means non-uniform. Slope of a velocity–time graph = acceleration; area under a velocity–time graph = displacement. Nearly every graph question is one of these four.

10 · Stone thrown vertically upward

Chapter 2 · the textbook worked example, reused constantly

The three things being tested: at the highest point v = 0 (that is the whole trick); taking up as positive, a = −g on the way up; and the time up equals the time down, so total flight time is double. With u = 30 m/s and g = 10 m/s²: h = u²/2g = 45 m, tup = u/g = 3 s, total flight = 6 s.

1 · Structure of the atom — shells and electrons

Chapter 1 · the picture behind every configuration question

What to say: the nucleus holds the protons and neutrons and therefore almost all the mass. Electrons occupy shells K, L, M, N outward. Each shell holds a maximum of 2n² electrons (K = 2, L = 8, M = 18, N = 32), but the outermost shell never holds more than 8. The number of shells gives the period; the electrons in the outermost shell give the group and the family.

2 · Thomson's model vs Rutherford's model

Chapter 1 · Section I of the real 2025-26 paper had three questions on this

What to say: Thomson pictured the positive charge spread evenly through the atom with electrons embedded in it. Rutherford's gold foil experiment showed most alpha particles passed straight through, so the atom is mostly empty space, with all the positive charge and nearly all the mass in a tiny central nucleus, and electrons revolving around it. Bohr then added that electrons occupy fixed stationary energy levels. The neutron was discovered later, by Chadwick.

3 · How an ion is formed

Chapter 1 / 3 · a guaranteed 2-mark, and they want the equation

The equation is what earns the mark: Na → Na⁺ + e⁻. Metals lose their valence electrons, and the charge equals how many they lose (Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻, Al → Al³⁺ + 3e⁻). Non-metals gain (8 − valence) electrons: O + 2e⁻ → O²⁻, Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻. Every ion is chasing the configuration of its nearest noble gas — say which noble gas, because that is often part (b).

4 · Periodic table trends

Chapter 2 · atomic size was a full 3-mark question in the real paper

The two trends and their reasons: across a period size decreases — shells stay the same but nuclear charge grows, pulling electrons in. Down a group size increases — a new shell is added each time. Both the trend and the reason carry marks. Also know: Group 1 alkali metals, Group 2 alkaline earth metals, Groups 3–12 transition elements (last electron enters the penultimate shell), Group 17 halogens, Group 18 noble gases.

1 · 🗺️ India — the map-marking question

Geography Ch 1 · the SCERT model paper has a 4-mark map question on exactly these features

How to place them: the three main ranges run north-west to south-east in parallel arcs across the top. Himadri is innermost (northernmost), then Himachal, then Shiwalik closest to the plains. Karakoram sits beyond Himadri in the far north-west (Ladakh side), and the Naga Hills are in the far north-east near the Myanmar border. Learn the order inward-to-outward and the map question answers itself.

2 · 🗺️ Himalayas — north–south cross-section

Geography Ch 1 · the picture behind the range, height, Dun and Bugyal questions

What to say: the three ranges lie parallel and get lower southwards — Himadri is permanently snow-capped, Himachal is the middle band where most hill stations sit, and Shiwalik is the low outer range meeting the plains. Duns are the flat valleys between Himachal and Shiwalik. Bugyals are alpine meadows above the tree line, and Karewas are the humus-rich lake terraces of the Kashmir valley where saffron is grown.

3 · 🗺️ Northern plains — the belts and the vanishing river

Geography Ch 2 · answers Bhabar, Tarai, Bhangar, Khadar and the alluvial fan in one picture

The mechanism to state: a river leaving the mountains loses slope, so it loses velocity and drops its load — coarsest first. That builds the alluvial fan and sorts the plain into belts. The Bhabar is so porous the river sinks into it; it re-emerges in the Tarai as marsh. Beyond that lies the true alluvial plain: Bhangar above flood level (older, less fertile, kankar nodules) and Khadar on the floodplain (newer, resilted every flood, highly fertile).

4 · 📜 History timeline — where everything sits

History Ch 1–2 · stops you confusing Mahajanapadas with the Mauryan Empire

The confusion this fixes: Mahajanapadas are the 16 states of the 6th century BCE; Magadha was one of them. The Mauryan Empire came after, absorbing them. So Bimbisara and Ajatashatru are Magadha kings, while Chandragupta and Ashoka are Mauryan emperors — a favourite trap in matching questions.

5 · 📜 How the Constitution divides power — two different splits

Civics Ch 4 · federalism is vertical, separation of powers is horizontal

Don't mix these up — exams ask about both, sometimes in the same paper. Federalism divides power between levels of government. Separation of powers divides it between organs of government. The Union List exists because some things must be uniform nationwide; the State List because some things need local knowledge; the Concurrent List because some things genuinely belong to both.

1 · Chloroplast — grana and stroma

Ch 1 · a 3-mark structure question in the SCERT model paper

What to say: the grana are stacks of disc-shaped thylakoids holding the chlorophyll — this is where light is trapped and water is split (photolysis), releasing oxygen. The stroma is the surrounding fluid, where the energy from the light phase is used to convert CO₂ into glucose. The grana supply the energy; the stroma spends it.

2 · Villus — how absorption actually works

Ch 2 · the capillary/lacteal split is the marking point

Two things earn marks: villi are finger-like projections that hugely increase the surface area for absorption; and each villus has two absorptive routes — the blood capillaries take glucose and amino acids to the hepatic portal vein and the liver, while the lacteal takes fats into the lymphatic system, bypassing the liver at first.

3 · The heart — two sides, two circuits

Ch 2 · the 4-mark question in the SCERT model paper

The design logic: the right side handles deoxygenated blood (body → lungs, pulmonary circulation) and the left handles oxygenated blood (lungs → body, systemic circulation). The septum keeps them separate so tissues get fully oxygenated blood. The left ventricle's wall is thicker because it must pump to the whole body, not just next door to the lungs.
The catch examiners love: the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood and the pulmonary vein carries oxygenated blood — because artery and vein are named by direction relative to the heart, not by oxygen content.

4 · Tooth structure

Ch 2 · a 3-mark "redraw and label" question in the SCERT model paper

Four layers, outside in: enamel (hardest non-living substance in the body), dentine (bone-like, contains calcium, forms the bulk), pulp cavity (nerves and blood vessels — this is what hurts in a cavity), and cementum covering the root and anchoring the tooth in the jaw. The exam version asks you to identify "the hard non-living part" (enamel) and "the connective tissue containing calcium" (dentine).
